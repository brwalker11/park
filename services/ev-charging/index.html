EV Charging

Find out whether your property can support charging before anyone quotes it

Most EV charging conversations start with equipment. They should start with the site. Available electrical capacity, driver demand in the area, and accessibility requirements decide whether a project is viable, and all three can be answered before a provider is involved.

Where It Meets Parking

Your lot is already the asset

A property owner evaluating charging is usually evaluating it in isolation. How many ports, which provider, where they go.

That skips the question that matters. Charging occupies parking spaces. Those spaces already have a use, a turnover pattern, and in many cases a revenue model. Putting a charger in the wrong stall converts a space that earns into a space that sits idle while one vehicle charges for four hours.

An assessment that covers parking, lighting and charging together sees this. It knows which stalls turn over, where drivers actually arrive from, which corner of your lot is dead, and what enforcement is already in place to stop a charging stall from being blocked by a vehicle that is not charging.

Charging placed against that picture is a different decision from charging placed against a site map.

Site Viability

Three things decide it, and none of them are the charger

Available power. This is the one that ends most projects. Charging draws significant load, and many commercial properties are already close to the capacity of their existing service. Where capacity is short, the project includes a service upgrade and utility coordination, and both the cost and the timeline can exceed the charging equipment itself. Distance from the panel to the proposed stalls matters for the same reason, because the run has to be trenched.

Demand in the area. A charger with no drivers is an expense with a warranty. Viability depends on EV density around your property, what charging already exists nearby, and whether the people using your lot are dwelling long enough to charge. A site that works for a hotel or an office may not work for a lot with twenty minute turnover.

Accessibility and permitting. EV charging at a commercial property is generally treated as a place of public accommodation, which brings accessible stall, clear floor space and accessible route requirements into the design rather than leaving them as an afterthought. Permitting varies substantially by jurisdiction, and some states have adopted their own installation requirements on top of local review.

Any of the three can disqualify a site. Finding out which one applies takes a site evaluation, not a quote.

Before You Sign

The questions that decide how this goes

Charging agreements vary more than the equipment does. Two providers offering the same hardware can offer very different terms, and the differences show up years later rather than at signing.

Worth establishing before anything is signed:

  • Who pays for installation, and what happens to that equipment at the end of the term
  • Who carries ongoing maintenance, and what the response time is when a port fails
  • How your property is compensated, and whether that changes with utilization
  • Who sets pricing at the charger, and whether your property has any say
  • What happens if the provider exits the market or is acquired
  • How long the term runs, and what the exit looks like

None of these are unusual questions. They are simply easier to ask before a proposal arrives than after.

Vendor Neutral

Assessment first, introductions second

The role here is narrow and worth stating plainly.

A site evaluation covering power, demand, placement and accessibility, delivered as an answer rather than a proposal. Where charging makes sense, an introduction to providers who fit your property, with no product line to protect and no equipment to sell. Where it does not, that answer, with the reason.

There is no charging hardware to push, which means there is no reason to recommend charging at a site that will not support it.
